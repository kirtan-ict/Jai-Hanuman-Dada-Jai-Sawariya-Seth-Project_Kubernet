GPU and AI infrastructure

Keep expensive GPUs busy and models online

GPUs are the most expensive resource in most clusters and the easiest to waste. We build Kubernetes platforms that share accelerators fairly between training, fine-tuning and inference, serve models reliably, and show you exactly where the GPU hours go.

When this fits

  • Several teams compete for a limited pool of GPUs, in the cloud or on your own servers.
  • You serve open-weight or in-house models and need autoscaling, rollouts and observability.
  • Training and batch jobs need queueing, quotas and gang scheduling.
  • Data or models must stay in your environment rather than going to a hosted API.
  • GPU spend is significant and nobody can say how well it is used.

When it doesn't

  • You only call hosted model APIs and run no models yourself.
  • One data scientist runs occasional experiments; a single managed notebook or VM is cheaper and simpler.
  • Very large training runs that need specialised HPC scheduling and interconnects may be better on a dedicated HPC or managed training service.
Reference architecture

One GPU fleet, many workloads

The biggest savings rarely come from cheaper GPUs. They come from not leaving them idle: partitioning cards for small models, queueing jobs instead of reserving nodes, scaling inference down when traffic drops, and loading models quickly enough that scaling is practical.

  • Right-sized slices: MIG on supported data-centre GPUs, time-slicing for development.
  • Fair sharing: Kueue or Volcano queues with per-team quotas and borrowing.
  • Fast model loading: cached weights on local NVMe or shared storage near the GPUs.
  • Visible usage: DCGM metrics joined with team labels for showback.
Implementation

What we implement

  • GPU node pools on cloud (EKS, AKS, GKE) or bare metal, with the NVIDIA GPU Operator managing drivers, container toolkit, device plugin and DCGM exporter.
  • GPU sharing using Multi-Instance GPU (MIG) profiles and time-slicing, chosen per workload's isolation needs.
  • Batch scheduling with Kueue or Volcano: queues, quotas, priorities, preemption and gang scheduling.
  • ML platform components: Kubeflow Notebooks, Pipelines and training operators, or Ray clusters with KubeRay.
  • Model serving with KServe and vLLM (and other runtimes such as Triton where suitable), including autoscaling and canary rollouts.
  • Model and dataset storage: object storage, high-throughput file systems and local caches for fast cold starts.
  • GPU observability and cost reporting by team, project and model.
  • Security for AI workloads: isolated namespaces, network policies, secrets for model registries and signed model images.

GPU platforms add drivers, firmware and fast-moving ML frameworks to the usual cluster work. Our support plans cover all of it.

Patching

Model registry and pipeline metadata backups, versioned model artefacts, and serving endpoints that can be recreated in another region or cluster.

Scale-to-zero for idle inference, spot or preemptible GPUs for tolerant jobs, MIG profile tuning and monthly utilisation reviews.

Incident response

Failed GPUs, driver mismatches, out-of-memory errors, stuck jobs and inference latency spikes, triaged with GPU-aware runbooks.

Lessons learned

Common pitfalls we prevent

  • Whole GPUs reserved for notebooks that sit idle most of the day.
  • Drivers baked into node images by hand, drifting out of step with CUDA versions in containers.
  • Non-GPU pods landing on GPU nodes because taints and tolerations were missing.
  • Slow cold starts from downloading large model weights on every scale-up.
  • Distributed jobs deadlocking when only some of their pods get scheduled.
  • No per-team visibility, so GPU costs cannot be explained or controlled.

FAQ

GPU and AI questions

Related services: AI and ML platforms, FinOps, observability, and support.

MIG or time-slicing?
MIG splits supported data-centre GPUs into hardware-isolated instances with dedicated memory, which suits production inference of smaller models. Time-slicing lets several pods share a GPU without memory isolation, which is fine for development and light workloads but not for strict multi-tenancy.
Kueue or Volcano?
Kueue is a Kubernetes SIG project that adds job queueing and quotas on top of the default scheduler and integrates with many job types. Volcano is a full batch scheduler with rich gang scheduling and HPC-style features. Kueue is often the simpler starting point; Volcano suits heavier batch and HPC workloads.
Should we serve LLMs with vLLM or KServe?
They work together. vLLM is a high-throughput inference engine for large language models; KServe provides the Kubernetes serving layer around runtimes like vLLM, with autoscaling, routing and rollouts. Simpler setups can run vLLM as a plain Deployment.
Cloud GPUs or our own servers?
Cloud suits variable or experimental demand and avoids long hardware lead times. Owned servers can be much cheaper for steady, high utilisation. Many teams use both: a baseline on owned hardware and cloud capacity for peaks.
